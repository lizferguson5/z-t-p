Usual or unusual? Z, t, and the p-value

Every hypothesis test asks one question: is my result the kind of thing that happens by chance all the time, or is it unusual? These six tabs build that idea one step at a time, starting with the 68/95/99.7 rule and ending with real scenarios you look up in the tables yourself.

How to use this page

Work through the tabs in order, 1 to 6. Each tab matches a section of your Canvas questions. The lookup tables are always one click away from the orange Tables button in the bottom corner.

1The normal curve and the 68/95/99.7 rule

A forest survey has measured thousands of Douglas-fir trees in one stand. Their heights follow a normal curve with a mean of μ = 15 m and a standard deviation of σ = 2.5 m. The normal curve lets us say which heights are usual and which are unusual.

Where do most trees fall?

Click a button to light up that zone.

68% · within 1 SD

About 2 out of 3 trees are between 12.5 and 17.5 m. Totally ordinary.

95% · within 2 SD

Almost all trees are between 10 and 20 m. Only 5% are outside this zone, 2.5% on each side.

99.7% · within 3 SD

Practically every tree is between 7.5 and 22.5 m. Outside this is extremely rare.

Where does "1.96" come from? The "95% within 2 SD" rule is rounded. The exact line that holds the middle 95% is at 1.96 SD. So whenever you see 1.96, read it as: "the edge of the usual 95%. Past this line, a result is in the unusual outer 5%."

Turn any tree into a z-score, then a probability

A z-score says how many standard deviations a value is from the mean. Once you have z, one table (the Z-table, also called Phi or Φ) gives the probability.

2Z vs t: the same curve, with more caution

Biologists use the Z-distribution for two different jobs. The t-distribution only shows up in the second one.

Job 1 · One value → probability

"What's the chance a tree is taller than 20 m?" You know μ and σ for the population, so z = (x − μ) / σ and the Z-table gives the answer. This is what you did in Tab 1. Sample size doesn't matter here.

Always Z.

Job 2 · An average → test

"Is the average of my 9 trees different from 15 m?" Now the question is about a sample average. What matters is whether you know the true σ or only your sample's own SD (s).

σ known → Z. Only s → t.

Why does t need a wider "usual" zone?

When you estimate the spread from a small sample, chance results land far out more often. If you kept the Z line at ±1.96, too many ordinary results would be called "unusual." So t moves its line farther out. Slide n and watch.

3The test statistic and the p-value

A seed company's records say this tomato variety averages 50 cm at 6 weeks, with σ = 6 cm. You grow plants with a new fertilizer and measure their average height. Does the fertilizer change height?

Step 1 · HypothesesH₀: no real effect. Plants still average 50 cm.
HA: there is a real effect.
Pick α, usually 0.05.
Step 2 · Test statisticHow many standard errors away is my result from what H₀ predicts?
Step 3 · p-valueIf H₀ is true, how likely is a result this extreme or more?
p < α → reject H₀.

How to read this figure

The curve. Every test statistic you could get if H₀ were true, just from the luck of which plants you measured. It's the 68/95/99.7 curve again, centered on 0 ("exactly what H₀ predicts").
The bottom axis: "set of possible results." Measured in test statistics, that is, standard errors away from 50 cm. The second row shows the same spots in centimeters.
The height: "probability density." Tall in the middle means results near 50 cm are more likely observations. Low at the edges means far-out results are very unlikely observations.
The dot: "observed data point." Your actual result, converted into a test statistic so it can be placed on the curve.
The shaded area: the p-value. The share of the curve at your dot or farther out. That is the chance of a result this extreme or more if H₀ is true.
The dashed α lines. Where "unusual" starts. If your dot lands past them, the shaded area is smaller than α, so you reject H₀.

Try this

  1. Turn on "Show 68/95/99.7 zones." Keep n = 9 and slide the average from 50 up to 56. Stop when the dot leaves the middle 95%. What is the test statistic there, and what is p?
  2. Set the average to 53.5 cm with n = 9 (two-tailed, α = 0.05). Read the p-value and the plain-language sentence under the stats.
p is NOT the chance H₀ is truep = 0.04 does not mean "4% chance the fertilizer does nothing." It means: if H₀ is true, there's a 4% chance of data this extreme.
p is NOT the size of the effectWith enough plants, a tiny 1 cm difference can give a very small p. Always look at the actual difference too.
A large p does NOT prove "no effect"It means the difference could easily be natural variability. You "fail to reject" H₀; you never prove it.

4Ways to be wrong: Type I, Type II, and power

Every test ends with a decision, and the decision can be wrong in two different ways. Think of a fire alarm.

True state of nature ↓ · Your conclusion →
Fail to reject H₀
Alarm stays quiet
Reject H₀
Alarm goes off
H₀ is trueNo real effect (no fire)
Correct conclusionNothing happening, and you said so.
Type I error · false positiveA fire alarm going off when there is no fire. You claim an effect that isn't real.Chance = α (you choose it)
H₀ is falseReal effect (real fire)
Type II error · false negativeIgnoring the smoke and missing a real fire. A real effect goes undetected.Chance = β
Correct conclusion · powerYou caught a real effect.Chance = 1 − β

Memory trick: Type I = "I see something that isn't there." Type II = "I'm too blind to see what IS there."

The trade-off. Lowering α (your threshold) from 0.05 to 0.01 reduces false positives but raises false negatives. Use the stricter α = 0.01 when a false positive is costly, such as approving a dangerous drug or diagnosing a fatal disease.
